3. Before you send

Send it so you can prove it

Use a method that gives you proof: certified mail with a return receipt, or whatever delivery method your lease requires for notices. Email too if your landlord uses it. Keep a copy of every letter and every receipt.

Document everything

Take dated photos and videos of the problem, or of the unit at move-out. Keep texts, emails, and repair receipts. Write down the date and the name of everyone you talk to.

Don't stop paying rent on your own

Some states let tenants withhold rent, pay into court, or "repair and deduct," but only by following exact steps. Doing it wrong can lead to eviction. Check your state's rules, or ask legal aid, first.

Call the local inspector for serious problems

For serious health or safety problems, many cities and counties have a housing or code enforcement office that can inspect. Search your city or county website for "code enforcement" or "housing inspection." For mold, see the EPA's mold page.

Never ignore court papers

If you get an eviction lawsuit or court summons, get legal help right away. Deadlines in eviction cases can be very short, and missing one can mean losing automatically.

Keep it calm

Facts, dates, and a clear request work better than anger. Assume the letter could become evidence.

4. General principles (check your state)

These are broad principles, not your state's exact rules. Look up your state through the official links below.

Security deposits

The laws of most states limit the size of a deposit, say what it can be used for, and set specific rules for when and how landlords must return it. California is one example: there, the deadline to return a deposit is 21 days after the tenant moves out. Deadlines and rules differ from state to state.

Source: Cornell Law School LII: security deposit.

Habitability

Most US jurisdictions recognize an "implied warranty of habitability": landlords must keep residential rentals safe and fit to live in, even if the lease doesn't mention repairs. Habitable generally means substantially complying with housing codes. Tenant remedies vary by state and can include repairs, court action, or (in some places) withholding rent.

Source: Cornell Law School LII: implied warranty of habitability.

Retaliation

The habitability doctrine is often linked to protections against retaliatory eviction, which bar landlords from evicting or punishing tenants for reporting housing code violations. Whether and how this applies depends on your state and city.

Source: Cornell LII.

Discrimination

If you believe you've experienced housing discrimination, you can file a complaint with HUD's Office of Fair Housing and Equal Opportunity.

Source: HUD: File a fair housing complaint.

HUD-insured or HUD-managed buildings

If HUD insures or manages your property, you can report the landlord's negligence or fraud to HUD's Multifamily Housing Complaint Line.

Sources: USA.gov: tenant rights; HUD Multifamily Housing Complaint Line.

Find your state's rules

USA.gov's tenant-rights page links to HUD's list of state tenant-rights resources, which can include your attorney general, housing agency, or state tenant handbook. Your state attorney general's consumer office is another good source.

5. Free legal help

  • LawHelp.org: find nonprofit legal aid and self-help information for your state: lawhelp.org
  • Legal Services Corporation: find an LSC-funded legal aid office near you: lsc.gov
  • LawHelp Interactive: free court forms and legal documents for housing and other issues: lawhelpinteractive.org
  • ABA Free Legal Answers: ask a volunteer lawyer a civil legal question online, if you qualify: abafreelegalanswers.org
