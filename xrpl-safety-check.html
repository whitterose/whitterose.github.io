3. XRPL scams to know

"Sign this to claim your airdrop" (account takeover)

The most damaging XRPL scams don't need your seed. They ask you to sign a SetRegularKey, SignerListSet, or AccountSet that disables your master key. After that, the scammer controls your account. Paste any sign request into section 2 first.

Missing destination tag

Exchanges often use one address for many customers and tell deposits apart by the destination tag. Send without the tag (or with the wrong one) and your deposit may be delayed or lost. Real exchanges show the tag on the same deposit page.

Address poisoning and lookalike addresses

Scammers send tiny payments from addresses that start and end like one you use, hoping you'll copy theirs from your history. Compare the whole address, or use your wallet's saved contacts.

Junk tokens and trust line bait

Spam tokens and "claim" offers push you to set a trust line to an unknown issuer, often with a website in the token name or memo. Trust lines also tie up part of your XRP reserve. Only add trust lines to issuers you've checked yourself.

"Activation" and "unlock" fees

A new XRPL account needs a small reserve, set by validator vote and shown on xrpl.org. Anyone charging an extra "activation", "unlock", or "verification" fee to release your XRP is scamming you.

Fake Xaman and wallet support

No real support team will DM you first, ask for your family seed or secret numbers, or ask you to sign something to "fix" your wallet.

Partial payments (for merchants)

A payment with the partial-payment flag can deliver far less than its Amount field shows. If you accept XRPL payments, always check delivered_amount in the result, not Amount.

Free NFT "offers"

A sell offer for your NFT at 0 XRP, or a strange brokered offer, can give your NFT away. Read the amount and the destination before you accept or create NFT offers.
