1. Check a message

Paste the message, or type what the caller said as closely as you can remember. Removing your own name, account numbers, and other personal details first is fine; they aren't needed.

Also tick anything that's true (it may not show up in the words):

How this works: the page looks for words and patterns linked to common scams. It can't know who really sent a message, and scammers change their wording all the time. A message can be a scam even if nothing is flagged. When in doubt, contact the company or agency yourself, using a phone number or website you already know is real (not one from the message). That advice comes from the FTC.

2. Already paid or shared something? Act fast.

It's not your fault. Scammers are professionals. The sooner you act, the better your chances. These steps come from the FTC's What To Do if You Were Scammed.

Paid with a gift card

Contact the gift card company right away, using the number on the back of the card. Tell them it was a scam and ask for a refund. Keep the card and receipt.

Wire transfer (Western Union, MoneyGram)

Contact the wire transfer company immediately. Tell them a scammer tricked you, and ask them to reverse the transfer and refund your money.

Zelle, bank transfer, or a payment app

Report it to your bank or the app (Venmo, Cash App, PayPal…) immediately, and ask them to reverse the payment.

Credit or debit card

Call the number on the back of your card. Ask them to reverse the charge. Federal law gives card users some protection against unauthorized charges.

Cryptocurrency or a crypto ATM

Contact the exchange or ATM operator immediately and ask them to reverse the transaction. Then report it at ReportFraud.ftc.gov and to the FBI at ic3.gov. Crypto payments are usually hard to get back (FTC).

Mailed cash

Call the U.S. Postal Inspection Service at 1-877-876-2455 right away. For a fee, they may be able to intercept a package that hasn't been delivered yet.

Gave your Social Security number

Go to IdentityTheft.gov to report it and get a personal recovery plan. Social Security also suggests placing a fraud alert with the credit bureaus (SSA).

Gave a password or a code

Change that password right away (and anywhere else you used it) and turn on two-step verification. If you're locked out, use the company's official account recovery page.

Let someone into your computer

Update your security software, run a scan, and delete anything it flags. Then change your passwords and turn on two-step verification.

Someone offers to "recover" your money

Be very careful. Recovery offers that charge a fee are a common second scam. The FBI says IC3 never contacts victims directly and doesn't work with law firms or crypto services to recover funds (IC3; FTC).

3. Report it (official places)

Reporting helps stop scammers, even if you didn't lose money. Not sure where? USA.gov's "Where to report a scam" tool asks a few questions and points you to the right agency.

  • Federal Trade Commission: ReportFraud.ftc.gov, the main place to report scams. Phone: 1-877-382-4357 (press 3 for an interpreter, per SSA).
  • FBI Internet Crime Complaint Center: ic3.gov, for online and cyber-enabled fraud, including crypto, romance, and investment scams.
  • Scam texts: forward them to 7726 (SPAM). Phishing emails: forward them to reportphishing@apwg.org (FTC).
  • Someone pretending to be Social Security: oig.ssa.gov. Pretending to be the IRS: IRS reporting page.
  • Age 60 or older? The U.S. Justice Department's National Elder Fraud Hotline: 1-833-FRAUD-11 (1-833-372-8311), Mon–Fri, 10 a.m.–6 p.m. Eastern. English, Spanish, and other languages. A case manager can help you report (OVC).
  • Identity theft: IdentityTheft.gov.
  • Unwanted sales calls: register and report at DoNotCall.gov.
  • Your local police, especially if you lost money or someone came to your home. Social Security's scam page also suggests this (SSA).
  • Free, non-government help talking it through: AARP Fraud Watch Network Helpline, 877-908-3360, Mon–Fri, 8 a.m.–8 p.m. Eastern. Open to anyone (AARP).

If someone is in immediate danger, call 911. For abuse or neglect of an older adult, contact Adult Protective Services or the Eldercare Locator at 1-800-677-1116 (DOJ Elder Justice).

4. Protect your parents (or anyone you love)

Scammers often target older adults. The FBI runs a dedicated elder fraud page (IC3). A calm 20-minute conversation and a card by the phone can make a real difference.

How to bring it up

  • Make it about scammers, not about them: "These people are professionals, and they fool everyone. I want us both to be ready."
  • Share a scam that almost fooled you. People rarely talk about scams because they're embarrassed.
  • Agree that they can call you anytime, about anything, with no judgment. Shame is what keeps people quiet.
  • Practice: "If someone calls saying they're from Medicare, what will you do?" → "Hang up and call the number on my card." The FTC notes that no government agency will ask you to pay over the phone (FTC).

Set things up together

  • Pick a family safe word that only you know. The FCC suggests this for "grandparent" scams that may use AI-cloned voices (FCC). Don't write it on the fridge card.
  • Save real numbers in their phone: family, the bank (from the back of the card), and their doctor.
  • Turn on call and text filtering through their phone settings or carrier (FTC).
  • Ask if they'd like a my Social Security account. SSA says having one can help block identity thieves from opening one in their name (SSA).

STOP. It might be a scam.

  1. Hang up. Don't click. Don't call back the number they gave you. Call using a number you already know is real.
  2. Nobody real asks for payment in gift cards, wire transfers, cryptocurrency, payment apps, gold, or cash. If they do, it's a scam. (FTC, SSA)
  3. Government agencies (IRS, Social Security, Medicare, courts) won't call to threaten arrest or demand payment over the phone. (FTC, IRS, SSA)
  4. Never share a code sent to your phone, a password, or your Social Security or bank numbers with anyone who contacted you.
  5. Pop-up says "virus, call this number"? It's fake. Real security warnings never ask you to call. (FTC)
  6. "Grandma, I'm in trouble, don't tell Mom"? Hang up, call them at their usual number, and ask for our safe word. (FTC, FCC)
  7. Told to keep it secret or to hurry? That's the scam talking. Stop and call someone you trust.
