3. Tips for applying

Gather documents

Offices tell you what they need, but it commonly includes: ID for each person applying, proof of address (like a recent bill), and proof of income (recent pay stubs, a benefits letter, or your latest tax return). If you're already enrolled in SNAP, Medicaid, or TANF, bring proof. For WIC, that may show you're already income-eligible.

Source: USDA: How to apply for WIC.

SNAP timing and interviews

You apply in the state where you live. In most cases the state decides within 30 days, after an interview (usually by phone or in person). Some very-low-income households can get benefits within 7 days. If you're approved, benefits count from the day you applied, so apply early, even if you're missing a document.

Source: USDA SNAP eligibility.

Denied? You can appeal

For SNAP, you can ask for a fair hearing within 90 days of a decision you disagree with, by phone, in writing, or in person. Other programs have their own appeal rights, explained in your notice. Read every letter you get, and note the deadlines.

Source: USDA SNAP eligibility.

Rules changed in 2025–2026

USDA says the One Big Beautiful Bill Act of 2025 changes certain SNAP eligibility factors, including work requirements and non-citizen eligibility. HealthCare.gov notes that a law change narrowed which immigration statuses qualify for full Medicaid and CHIP. Always check current rules with your state.

Sources: USDA; HealthCare.gov.

Never pay to apply

Applying for these government programs is free. Be wary of anyone who charges a fee or "guarantees" approval. For example, HHS warns that LIHEAP doesn't charge a fee and doesn't give direct grants to individuals.

Source: HHS/ACF LIHEAP.

Ask for help applying

Call 211 or visit 211.org for local help. USDA says that if you can't get to a SNAP office, someone else can act as your authorized representative if you designate them in writing.

Source: USDA SNAP eligibility.
