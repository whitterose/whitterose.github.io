3. What to do now

First: being scammed is not your fault. These are organized, professional operations that fool smart, careful people every day. Acting quickly matters more than how it happened.

Never share your seed phrase

Your seed phrase (recovery phrase) or private key is your wallet. Anyone who has it can take everything. No support team, exchange, airdrop, app, or "validator" will ever need it. Anyone asking for it is stealing.

Seed phrase exposed? Move funds now

If you typed it into a website or shared it with anyone, treat the wallet as lost. Create a brand-new wallet with a new seed phrase (on a device you trust), and move whatever is left to it right away. Revoking approvals won't help here: they have your keys. Stop using the old wallet.

Signed an approval? Revoke it

On Ethereum and other EVM chains, look up your address in revoke.cash or Etherscan's Token Approval Checker and revoke anything you don't recognize. On Solana, use your wallet's own tools: Solflare lists token delegations and can revoke them, and Phantom's help center describes revoking token allowances. Advanced users can use spl-token revoke. Type these names into your browser yourself. Don't click links someone sent you. Revoking costs a small network fee.

Lost money on a "platform" or app?

Stop sending money. Paying a "withdrawal fee", "tax", or "unlock deposit" never gets your funds out. It's how the scam takes more. Save screenshots, wallet addresses, transaction IDs (hashes), usernames, and website addresses.

Report it

  • FBI Internet Crime Complaint Center: ic3.gov
  • FTC: ReportFraud.ftc.gov
  • The platform: use the report button on X, Discord, or Telegram, and tell the exchange you sent from (through its app or website).
  • Outside the US? Report to your national police or fraud agency too.

Reports to IC3 and the FTC help investigators link cases, even when they can't get your money back.

Beware of "recovery" offers

After a loss, you'll likely be contacted by "hackers", "blockchain investigators", "law firms", or even fake government agents who promise to get your crypto back for a fee. That's a second scam. The FBI says IC3 doesn't contact victims to offer recovery services (IC3; FTC).

If this loss is weighing on you

Losing money, especially savings, can feel crushing, and shame makes it worse. You're not stupid and you're not alone. Talk to someone you trust. If you're in emotional crisis or thinking about hurting yourself, in the US you can call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, 988lifeline.org), any time. If you're in immediate danger, call 911 or your local emergency number.

4. Common crypto scams, in plain words

Wallet drainers and "connect wallet to claim"

A fake site (often promoted as an airdrop, a mint, or a refund) asks you to connect, then pushes a signature that hands over your tokens or NFTs. One click can empty a wallet.

Approval and permit phishing

"Approve", "permit", "set approval for all", and gasless signatures can let someone else move your tokens later, even after you close the site. Read every wallet pop-up. If you don't understand it, reject it.

Seed phrase requests ("validate your wallet")

"Your wallet needs to be synced, validated, rectified, or migrated. Enter your phrase." It's always theft.

Fake support on X, Discord, and Telegram

You post a question and "support" or an "admin" DMs you first, or sends you to a "ticket" site. Real teams don't DM first and never ask for your phrase or to connect your wallet to fix something.

Recovery scams

People who already lost money are targeted again by fake recovery services that charge upfront fees.

Pig butchering and romance investing

A friendly stranger (often from a "wrong number" text or a dating app) builds trust over weeks, then shows you a "trading platform" with amazing gains. Withdrawals get blocked with fees and "taxes". See the SEC on relationship investment scams.

Fake exchanges and apps with withdrawal fees

Your balance looks great, but to withdraw you must pay a "tax", an "AML fee", or a "deposit to unlock". It's never released.

Pump and dump, honeypot, and rug-pull tokens

Hype ("next 100x", "get in early") pushes the price up while insiders sell. Honeypots are coded so you can buy but not sell. If mint authority is active, more tokens can be printed. If liquidity isn't locked or burned, it can be pulled. See the SEC on pump-and-dump schemes.

Giveaway doubling ("send 1, get 2")

No one sends double back. Often uses deepfake livestreams of famous people.

Impersonated celebrities and companies

The FTC says celebrities aren't contacting you on social media to multiply your crypto (FTC).

Address poisoning

Scammers send tiny or zero-value transfers from an address that looks like one you use (same first and last characters), hoping you'll copy it from your history next time. Always check the full address, or use your wallet's address book.

Fake job and task scams paid in USDT

"Rate apps or products, earn commission in USDT." Soon you must "recharge" or top up your own money to unlock "combo tasks". The FTC says never pay to get a job.
